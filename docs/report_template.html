2Motivation: whose problem this solves

Who has the problem. Anyone meeting game theory for the first time: undergraduates and MBA students in intro economics, strategy, and negotiation courses (like my MGT 409 classmates), and also middle- and high-school students. Strategic thinking (cooperation, betrayal, fairness, bargaining) is intuitive to teenagers, but it's usually locked behind college math notation. GTiA needs no math beyond comparing two numbers, uses cartoon characters and a playful heist story, and is teen-safe by design (§6), so a teacher can hand it to a 12-year-old. The instructors who teach these students are users too.

How they handle it now. A lecture shows a 2×2 payoff matrix, the instructor circles the dominant strategy and the Nash equilibrium, and students do problem sets. Some instructors run live classroom experiments, which take class time, setup, and a full room of participants.

What it costs them. Time: a live classroom experiment takes a class session, setup, and a full room of participants, so most students never get one; a student who misses the idea has no opponent to practice against at 11 p.m. Money: experiment platforms charge per seat, and one-on-one tutoring costs far more than the ~$0.10 per student-month GTiA costs to run (§5). Frustration and misunderstanding: the concepts are abstract and the matrix is handed to students rather than discovered, so the classic confusion survives: mixing up "the outcome that is best for both of us" with "the choice that is best for me no matter what the other person does." My very first tester showed exactly this: after playing an early version they rated their confidence 5/5 but scored 1/5 on the concept check, choosing cooperative "best for both" answers for dominant strategy and Nash equilibrium (§7). Confidence without understanding is the expensive failure mode: it walks into the exam, the negotiation, and the pricing decision.

Why anyone should care. The Prisoner's Dilemma and bargaining are everywhere: price wars, arms races, climate agreements, group projects, salary negotiations. People who can spot a dominant strategy, or see why an "unfair" offer gets rejected, make better decisions as managers, voters, and teammates, and teenagers can learn it years before college.

The bet. If students live the dilemma (sit in the interrogation chair, get betrayed, fill in the payoff themselves, and then have to name the dominant strategy before anyone tells them), the concept sticks. AI characters make that possible for a single student at any time, with no classroom of human opponents required.

What GTiA does differently

  • You build the matrix yourself. Each round lands you in a different cell; you type the years for you and Tung Tung Tung Sahur. A deterministic coverage controller guarantees all four cells in at most four rounds, whatever the player does.
  • AI characters with roles, not one chatbot. A cop who interrogates and tells the story, a partner with memory and personality who (in Door 3) really decides whether to accept your offer, and a tutor who coaches without giving away answers.
  • Code is the referee, AI is the voice. Every sentence, payoff, score and grade is computed in code; the LLMs only talk and make in-character choices inside code-enforced limits.
  • Retrieval practice built in. Before the debrief you must name the dominant strategy (Door 1) or solve the backward-induction question (Door 3); then the matrix animates the answer.
  • Learning is measured. A before/after check, per-tester logs, and transcripts are part of the product (§7).
  • Teen-safe and game-like. A content filter screens everything players type and everything the AI says (§6), and an original procedural soundtrack, character leitmotifs, points, streaks, and badges keep younger players engaged.

Honest gaps: The Evolution of Trust is more polished and covers repeated games; MobLab has real multiplayer and teacher tools. GTiA is a single-player prototype with two games and three testers.

4The agents

GTiA has two playable doors off Equilibrium Hall: Door 1, Dilemma Detectives (Prisoner's Dilemma) and Door 3, The Ultimatum (bargaining and backward induction). Three AI characters play six agent roles. All are pydantic_ai.Agents with typed Pydantic outputs, a prompt file each (backend/prompts/*.md), and only read-only tools.

How they are organized

There is no LLM "boss". A deterministic Python orchestrator (state machine) decides whose turn it is, which agent to call, and computes every number. Agents never call each other. This keeps the game cheap, predictable, and testable, and it means a prompt can never talk the game into a different outcome.

Model choice: gpt-6-luna via Portkey

  • Why this model: it's the course-provided model, it's fast (mean 1.1–1.9 s per call measured), and the task (short in-character lines plus structured outputs) doesn't need a large reasoning model.
  • reasoning_effort="none": required for function/structured-output calls on this endpoint, and it keeps latency and output tokens low (mean ~90 output tokens per call).
  • Structured outputs everywhere: the frontend never parses free text; it receives CopLine, PartnerDecision, UltOffer and so on.

The coverage controller: the core pedagogical trick

In the Prisoner's Dilemma the player picks the row and the partner picks the column. To guarantee all 4 cells in ≤4 rounds: (1) if both cells in the player's row are empty, the partner agent chooses freely; (2) if one is filled, the partner is constrained to the other, and the output validator rejects any other move (the model retries; if it still refuses, the engine overrides it and logs it); (3) once a row is complete, that button greys out ("Already explored") and the tutor nudges the player to try the other strategy. A parametrized test plays every possible partner choice for a stubborn player and asserts 4 cells in 4 rounds.

A real run, step by step (Door 1)

Captured from a real game against the live model (screenshots from output/screenshots; one of three testers' sessions is quoted where noted).

1
Equilibrium Hall. The player walks (WASD) to Door 1. Manga posters drawn in code show their avatar with Tung Tung Tung Sahur pulling the heist.
2
Before-check. One rotating question (least-asked first) to measure baseline understanding (§7).
3
Interrogation. cop agent → CopLine: Detective Zhou explains the deal in speech bubbles the player clicks through. Tung Tung Tung Sahur is visibly terrified in Room B behind the glass, being questioned by a second officer.
4
Partner decides. The orchestrator computes the allowed partner moves → partner agent → PartnerDecision → validator → engine computes the sentence → cop announces it.
5
Cells + matrix. The camera glides to the jail cells. tutor → "Enter your years and Tung Tung Tung Sahur's years." The engine grades the numbers; on a miss, the tutor hints without revealing.
6
Rounds 2–4. Each repeat round gets a twist and prop (polygraph, donut box, red alarm) and short intros. Points, streaks, and badges are scored in code.
7
Dominant-strategy challenge. The tutor asks (fixed text appended in code): "Which is YOUR dominant strategy, Stay Silent or Confess? Look at the matrix… Ask me anything first." The player can type up to 3 questions. Tester 3 asked directly whether "confess" was correct; the tutor did not reveal it and instead walked them through comparing years down each column.
8
Highlight + debrief. The pick is graded in code. The matrix animates in five steps: your 0 < 1 and 2 < 3 → the Confess row is YOUR dominant strategy → the same for him → (2, 2) is the Nash equilibrium and (1, 1) is "best for both". Then the final score card and the after-check.

Door 3: The Ultimatum (the AI really decides)

In a bright, spotless King's Cross, you and Tung Tung Tung Sahur split 10,000 stolen H100s over 4 alternating rounds. In a real run, the AI rejected a 1,000 offer ("1,000 is an insult, Lost Property it is!"), offered 3,000 and then 4,000, and later remembered the insult ("remembers that 1,000 insult, but 4,000 is fair. Deal."). The backward-induction challenge then asks what a purely GPU-maximizing responder should accept (any offer above 0, so the proposer offers 500), and the debrief contrasts that prediction, (9,500, 500), with what the two of them actually did, on a game-tree diagram.

7Impact: evidence of usefulness

Method. Three classmates/friends played Door 1 on my laptop (Oct 24–25). Before Door 1 each answered one concept question (rotated); after the debrief they answered all five (including the same one) with the payoff matrix visible, then rated confidence and usefulness.   • The concept this game exists to teach moved. Tester #3 started with the classic misconception, answering that a dominant strategy is "always cooperating with your partner". In the game they missed the challenge once, asked the tutor two questions, found Confess, watched the highlight, and then answered dominant strategy correctly on the after-check (4/5 overall).
  • Learning in about 10–13 minutes. Testers went from before-check to after-check in 9.6–12.6 minutes; usefulness was rated 5/5 by all three.
  • Design changed because of users. Tester #1 (v1) filled every matrix cell correctly but scored 1/5 on concepts, picking "best for both" answers, while rating confidence 5/5. That led to: showing the matrix on the after-check (v2); removing payoffs from answer options after tester #2 said they made it too easy (v3); and the in-game "name your dominant strategy" challenge plus animated highlight (v4). Tester #1's transcript also exposed the tutor bug ("all four cells filled" after two), now fixed.
  • Unprompted transfer. Tester #2's free-text feedback (not consented for quotation) connected the Nash equilibrium to arms races, the real-world example from the debrief.
How to read this honestly. n = 3, self-selected friends, and three different versions: this is a pilot, not proof. Showing the matrix on the after-check (v2+) makes the best-response questions easier, so the concept questions (dominant strategy, Nash, dilemma) are the fairer evidence. Self-rated confidence was 5/5 even when understanding was 1/5, a reminder to trust the scores, not the ratings.

8Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Evidence is thin: three testers, no control group (for example, the same students reading a lecture slide), same questions before and after, one before-question per person.
  • The partner effectively "sees" your move in Door 1 so the coverage controller can guarantee all four cells. That breaks the simultaneity of a real Prisoner's Dilemma, a deliberate trade-off for teaching that the debrief should state.
  • Costs use a blended account rate, not exact gpt-6-luna input/output/cached prices.
  • Local only: game sessions live in memory; study data is a JSON-lines file, not a database; no accounts.
  • Door 2 (Stag Hunt) is locked; Door 3 has only been played by me so far, not by testers.
  • The teen-safe filter is word-list based: it catches profanity and common disguises, but not every creative spelling or mean-but-clean message, and it is English-only. The 988 referral is US-specific. A school deployment would add a moderation model.
  • Laptop-only (keyboard and mouse), English-only, and the 3D scenes need a WebGL-capable browser.

What I would build next, with another month

The two highest-value next steps, in order:

  1. A teacher dashboard (top priority). Everything a teacher needs is already in the logs: each student's before/after answers, which matrix cells they missed on the first try, whether they found the dominant strategy, what they asked the tutor, and how they bargained in Door 3. The dashboard would show, per class: which concept most students still miss (e.g. "60% confuse 'best for both' with 'dominant'"), each student's before → after change, time spent, and flagged sessions (blocked messages by category, never content). Teachers could assign a door as homework and see results the next morning, which turns GTiA from a game into a classroom tool. It needs a real data layer (SQLite → Postgres: participants, checks, answers, sessions, rounds, agent_runs) and a simple class code instead of accounts.
  2. More gamification for Door 3, The Ultimatum. Door 1 now has bets, points, streaks, badges, and round twists; Door 3 doesn't yet. Next: a GPU leaderboard (final haul and "GPUs lost to rejections"), a reputation meter that shows Tung Tung Tung Sahur's trust in you rising and falling with each offer, badges like "Fair Dealer" (every offer 40–60%), "Shark" (a lowball accepted), and "Backward Inductor" (challenge solved first try), round twists at King's Cross (a train delay that shrinks the pot, a rival thief), and a predict-the-response bet before he decides.

After those: a classroom pilot with a control group (lecture slide versus GTiA, same 5-question check a week later); Door 2, Stag Hunt, reusing the 2×2 engine; hosting with per-IP limits and a daily spend cap; and cost work (drop tool round-trips, measure exact cached-input pricing).
